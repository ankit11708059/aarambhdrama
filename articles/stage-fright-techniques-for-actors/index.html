Stage Fright: Why It Happens and 9 Techniques That Actually Help

Almost every performer gets nervous; the ones who look calm have simply learnt what to do with it. Here’s what stage fright is, and nine techniques that work in a rehearsal room and on opening night.

Your mouth goes dry. Your hands shake. Your heart is hammering and the first line — the one you've said a hundred times — has vanished. Stage fright is one of the most common experiences in performance, and plenty of seasoned performers say they still feel it before a show.

The good news: you don't have to make it disappear to perform well. You have to understand it and give it somewhere to go.

What's actually happening

Stage fright is your body's threat response switching on. Standing in front of an audience to be judged reads, to an old part of your brain, like danger. Adrenaline is released, the heart speeds up, breathing gets fast and shallow, blood moves to the big muscles (hence the shaking hands and wobbly knees), and digestion shuts down (hence the dry mouth and the butterflies).

None of this is a sign that you're bad at performing. It's a sign that the performance matters to you. The techniques below work on two fronts: calming the body's alarm where you can, and giving your attention a better job than worrying.

1. Call it excitement

The physical feelings of anxiety and excitement are remarkably similar: racing heart, alertness, energy. Psychologist Alison Wood Brooks at Harvard tested what happens when people facing a stressful performance — singing karaoke, giving a speech — simply say "I am excited" instead of trying to calm down. In her study, published in 2014, people who reframed their nerves as excitement performed better than those who told themselves to relax.

Trying to go from racing to calm is a long jump. Going from "anxious" to "excited" is a short step. Before you go on, say it — out loud if you can: I'm excited.

2. Breathe out longer than you breathe in

Fast, shallow breathing feeds panic. Slow breathing with a long exhale is one of the most reliable ways to settle your body down.

Try this in the wings: breathe in through the nose for a count of four, then out through the mouth for a count of six or eight, as if blowing through a straw. Five rounds. Feel your shoulders drop and your feet on the floor.

3. Use the energy physically

Adrenaline wants to be used. Instead of standing frozen backstage, shake it out: shake your hands, then arms, then legs, then the whole body, for thirty seconds. Do a few jumps. Stretch. A proper physical warm-up — and a vocal one like our 15-minute voice warm-up — burns off some of the excess and tells your body it's getting ready, not under attack.

4. Prepare until it's boring

A huge amount of stage fright is really the fear of forgetting. The cure is boring and it works: know your lines, cues and blocking so well that you could do them while thinking about something else. Run them in the shower, on the bus, while cooking. When the words are automatic, your attention is free for the scene — and the fear has much less to hold on to.

5. Put your attention on your partner

Nerves are a form of self-attention: How do I look? What are they thinking? The most effective cure is to give your attention a job outside yourself.

Stanislavski called it the circle of attention: when you feel exposed, shrink your focus to what's right in front of you — the cup in your hand, the face of the person you're talking to. Meisner trained it through listening: if you are genuinely watching your scene partner to see what they'll do next, there isn't room left to watch yourself. (See our guides to Stanislavski's system and the Meisner repetition exercise.)

On stage, your question should never be "how am I doing?" It should be "what does my character want from this person, and am I getting it?"

6. Build a pre-show routine

A fixed routine takes decisions — and therefore worry — out of the last hour. For example:

  1. Arrive early. Lateness doubles nerves.
  2. Physical warm-up, then vocal warm-up.
  3. Run your first scene's lines once, quietly.
  4. Five minutes alone: breathing, then thinking about your character's circumstances at the moment you enter.
  5. In the wings: "I'm excited."

Use the same routine for rehearsals, so by opening night it feels familiar rather than special.

7. Rehearse the first thirty seconds most

Nerves are usually worst just before you go on and in the first few moments of a scene. Once you're into it, the work takes over. So over-prepare your entrance and your first line: know exactly where you're coming from, what you want, and the first thing you'll do. Get the first thirty seconds right and the rest tends to follow.

8. Plan how you'll handle a mistake

Part of the fear is imagining what happens if it goes wrong. Decide in advance: if I lose a line, I'll stay in character, breathe, and say what the character would say. Audiences almost never notice a slip the actor doesn't show them. Knowing you have a plan for a mistake makes the mistake much less frightening — and much less likely.

9. Perform more, in small doses

Stage fright shrinks with exposure. You can't think your way out of it; you get used to being watched by being watched. Look for low-stakes chances: a reading for friends, an open mic, a scene shown at the end of a workshop, a short monologue at a community event. Each time, the alarm rings a little more quietly.

When it's more than nerves

For most people, stage fright is uncomfortable but manageable, and it fades with experience. If anxiety is stopping you from performing at all, causing panic attacks, or spilling into the rest of your life, talk to a doctor or a mental health professional. Don't self-medicate before shows — some people are prescribed medication for performance anxiety, but that is a decision for a doctor to make with you.

The nerves mean you care

The aim isn't to stop feeling anything before you go on. Many experienced actors say a performance without any nerves at all tends to go flat. Learn to read the racing heart as readiness, give your attention to the scene, and let the energy go into the work.
